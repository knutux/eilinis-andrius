More than thirty years after independence, Lithuania has made huge strides in many areas. Our military is NATO-ready. Our police are professionalized. Our tech sector has built a name in fintech and startups. We’ve embraced digital tools, European integration, and market competition.

And yet, walk into a typical public school or clinic, and something still feels... off.

It’s not just the furniture or fluorescent lighting. It’s the structure, the atmosphere, the quiet resignation on both sides of the desk. These systems still carry the imprint of Soviet-era thinking: centralized control, top-down management, and ritualized bureaucracy masquerading as order.

Education and healthcare have changed less than almost any other public sector. Why? Perhaps because they remained under strong state control. Perhaps because powerful internal actors — like rigid unions or hierarchical bureaucracies — resisted meaningful reform. Perhaps because these sectors don’t produce quick wins or exportable success stories, so they fall off the political radar.

The cost is real: demotivated professionals, young talent fleeing abroad, reformers burning out, and citizens losing trust in services that should represent the state at its most human.

Other countries have found ways to modernize these same systems without losing soul or equity. Could we? What would it take to break the inertia — and not just change the surface, but rebuild the culture?

This post is not a blueprint. It’s a question. But it’s time to stop pretending that these institutions will evolve on their own. Dust doesn’t lift itself.
