This post will tell the story of Futbolinis.lt — an ambitious project I worked on for more than a decade. It began not just from a love of football, but from a shift in how I wanted to spend my creative energy online.

I was once deeply involved in Lithuanian Wikipedia. For years, I tracked nearly every change made, helping shape and expand the project in its earliest days. But as it matured, it became too big to follow. I could no longer keep up without sacrificing both work and personal life. That’s when I quietly stepped away and turned toward something more personal — a hidden passion: sports statistics.

Football had roots in my family. It was my grandfather’s favorite sport. My father loved all sports and took me to unforgettable Žalgiris games near the end of the Soviet era. I remember standing in a crowd so large I couldn’t see the pitch, only feel the collective energy rise with every near-goal. The scent of cigarettes, the rhythm of chants, the jump of bodies — it stayed with me.

At the same time, a fellow Wikipedia contributor had wanted to upload massive amounts of European football data. I helped him, and in doing so realized: maybe I could build something that merged my wiki experience with my passion for football.

So in 2008, after around four years in Wikipedia, I started something new: an online project where Lithuanian football fans could visit their club’s page and find everything — all league seasons, results, key players, interlinked and structured like a living encyclopedia.

After a few months of focused work, I had a basic framework in place. The UI was clunky, the name “futbolinis” wasn’t elegant, but it worked — and it was flexible. I pulled stats from wherever I could: scraping sites, digging through scanned interwar newspapers, hunting for rare football books (there weren’t many).

I started collaborating with LFF (the Lithuanian Football Federation). One supportive person there would send me A league and I league match protocols after every season. I was even once invited to meet the LFF president, and at one point received a national team jersey as a thank-you. Others helped too — a few people from Vilnius region leagues, some lower-tier clubs, bits and pieces of encouragement.

But slowly I began to realize something: there just aren’t many football statistics nerds in Lithuania. The community I hoped would form never quite did. Volunteers didn’t come. The burden stayed mine alone.

I tried to keep it alive — even ran a semi-active Facebook page (https://www.facebook.com/futboloenciklopedija/) — but the energy it required kept growing. I couldn’t gather current stats, research history, and update the UI all at once.

Around 2016, after about eight years, I started to let go. The updates slowed. I knew it wouldn’t grow further — at least not with me behind it alone. But I still paid for hosting. I couldn’t quite bring myself to shut it down.

It quietly remained online for another eight years, until it finally disappeared in 2024.

This post will reflect not just on what I built, but on what it meant to _care_ about something few people saw. To put so much into a project that quietly existed in the background — and to eventually accept that letting go doesn’t mean it didn’t matter.
