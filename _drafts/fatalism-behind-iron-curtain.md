This post explores the quiet but pervasive fatalism that shaped life under Soviet rule — not as a spiritual concept, but as a learned survival strategy.

For most people, life was tightly controlled. Careers were assigned. Apartments were given. Travel was restricted. Dissent was dangerous. In such a system, ambition became not just useless, but potentially risky. The rational response was compliance. The emotional response was resignation.

Over time, this became a culture of fatalism — the deep-rooted belief that nothing you do can meaningfully change your path. Even if you were smart, hardworking, or idealistic, the system would not reward initiative. It might even punish it.

That kind of mental training doesn’t vanish overnight. Even after the fall of the USSR, its logic lingered. You can still feel it in places where state control remained dominant — especially in education and healthcare. Rigid hierarchy, box-checking procedures, and the unwritten rule: “Don’t try to fix what you can’t afford to break.”

Other sectors — like tech, entrepreneurship, and even parts of law enforcement or the military — modernized faster. Why? Perhaps because they were less entangled with the cultural legacy of resignation. Or perhaps because they rewarded agency, not obedience.

This post isn’t about nostalgia or blame. It’s about recognizing a mindset that was once rational — and asking whether we’re still carrying it today, even when it no longer serves us.

How do we build institutions, cultures, and habits that invite participation instead of passivity? What replaces resignation, once it becomes visible?

This post won’t give answers. But it will name a quiet force that still shapes how some of us think — even when the system it was built for is long gone.
